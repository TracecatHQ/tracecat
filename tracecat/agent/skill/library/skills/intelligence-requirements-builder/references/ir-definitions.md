# Intelligence Requirements: Definitions and Distinctions

Read this before generating any requirements. Correct terminology is a credibility signal in CTI; incorrect terminology is a credibility killer.

## What intelligence requirements are

Intelligence requirements (IRs) are the documented, agreed statements of what a CTI team's stakeholders need intelligence support on. The concept long predates commercial CTI: military and government intelligence organizations developed requirements as a way of pointing limited analytical resources at the outcomes that matter most. As CTI matured into a private sector profession, the discipline of writing requirements came with it, and adoption in commercial teams continues to grow.

IRs do two jobs at once:

1. They give the CTI team a sharp, agreed focus for its work.
2. They teach stakeholders what kinds of questions the team can take on, and at what depth, which over time shapes the asks that flow into the team's intelligence requirements list.

A useful framing: requirements are the bridge between day-to-day security work and what the business is trying to achieve.

## The two forms of IR

**Enduring requirements**, called either Standing Intelligence Requirements (SIRs) or General Intelligence Requirements (GIRs) depending on the team's convention. These are ongoing support needs with no fixed expiry. Most of a mature intelligence requirements list consists of these.

**Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs)**. These are the urgent tier. When one lands, the team typically reshuffles its attention and resourcing to deal with it. Key characteristics:

- They tend to enter the team's queue as unscheduled RFIs rather than through planned requirements gathering.
- They have a short shelf life: a PIR exists to support a near-term decision and retires once that decision is made.
- They are written to be answerable quickly, because the decision will not wait.

What separates a PIR from an enduring requirement is lifespan, not topic. The same subject can appear as both an enduring requirement and a PIR at different times.

## The distinction teams get wrong

**Prioritized intelligence requirements are not Priority Intelligence Requirements.**

Many teams rank their enduring requirements (weighing things like stakeholder demand, expected impact, feasibility, and available capacity) to decide which to serve first. The result is a *prioritized* list of enduring requirements. This is good practice, but it does not turn the top items into PIRs. A PIR is defined by its decision-bound, short-lived nature, not by its position in a ranking. The two concepts get mixed up regularly, both in published guidance and in practice; this skill must not mix them up.

When a user describes ranking their intelligence requirements list, call it prioritization. When a user describes a time-bound, decision-driven ask, that may be a PIR.

## Related concepts for mature teams

These break a requirement down into operational tasks and tend to appear in more developed CTI programs:

- **Essential Elements of Information (EEIs)**: the specific questions (the who, what, when, where, why, and how) an analyst must answer before a requirement can be considered satisfied. They break an enduring requirement into the precise data points to go and find, whether the team is tracking a threat actor across illicit forums and chat channels, following a malware campaign, or watching an emerging vulnerability. Finished deliverables map back to the EEIs they answer, which also gives the team a way to show its value against stakeholder priorities. EEIs are the bridge between a requirement and collection.
- **Collection Requirements**: which data sources need tasking to surface those facts.
- **Production Requirements**: which deliverables the team commits to producing, for whom, and on what cadence.

Offer these when the user signals maturity (mentions an intelligence requirements list, a collection management framework, or multiple analysts). Do not impose them on a team writing its first requirements.

## Capacity and prioritization

Once a set of requirements exists, someone has to decide which ones the team takes on. In practice this usually falls to the CTI manager and team lead together, with input from the analysts. The deciding factors include how much the team can carry today, what it would pick up next if headcount grew, and what it simply cannot serve because of gaps in skills, subject familiarity, or access to data and tooling.

Three approaches to ranking, usable individually or in combination:

1. **Time estimation.** Judge how long each requirement would take to research and turn into a finished product, given the backgrounds of the people on the team. Some requirements are quick wins; others are long hauls, and knowing which is which changes the order.
2. **Return on investment.** Rank by expected payoff against current security and business priorities. When several teams independently raise overlapping questions, that overlap is itself strong evidence of value.
3. **Stakeholder input.** Ask the people who submitted requirements to rank their own, and weigh that self-assessment into the final ordering.

Early in an intelligence program, proving value fast is often a goal in its own right, so requirements that can be answered quickly may reasonably rank above harder, higher-impact ones.
